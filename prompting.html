The other most-requested thing

Say what you mean

When I asked what people needed the most help with, this beat everything else, then it turned up again anyway, buried in the answers nobody prompted for it. So here's the whole thing properly, not a rushed paragraph at the bottom of another page.

Why your first go usually falls flat

Type "write me an email" and you'll get an email. A completely generic one, the kind anyone could have written, because that's all it had to go on. It isn't reading your mind, it's reading exactly what you typed, word for word. Vague in, vague out, every single time.

The four things a good prompt actually needs

Not a course, just these four. Use whichever ones are relevant, in whatever order makes sense for what you're asking.

Context

Tell it what it's working with

Who you are, what you're trying to do, what you've already tried. "I'm a nurse writing a handover note" gets you somewhere completely different to nothing at all on the subject.

Outcome

Say what good actually looks like

Not "help me plan a party", more "a party for twelve, in my flat, on a Saturday, that doesn't need me cooking on the day." The more specific the outcome, the less it has to guess.

Format

Tell it what shape you want back

A list, a table, three sentences, something you could read aloud. Say nothing and you'll usually get three paragraphs whether you wanted them or not.

Constraints

Give it the boundaries

A budget, a deadline, a tone, a length. "Keep it under 100 words" or "nothing over £50" does more work than another sentence of explanation.

Same task, two different prompts

Same person, same email, wildly different result depending on what you actually type.

Asking for help with a work email

The vague version:

Write me an email to my manager about needing more time.

The specific version:

I need an email to my manager, [name], asking for an extra week on the [project] deadline. I'm behind because [real reason]. I want to sound accountable, not like I'm making excuses. Keep it under 150 words and suggest a new date.

The first gets you something generic you'll end up rewriting anyway. The second gets you something close to what you'd actually send. Try both on the same thing and see the gap for yourself.

My honest take: you don't need to learn "prompt engineering", that's a job title, not a life skill. You just need to stop assuming it can read your mind, and actually tell it what you're picturing. More on why vague prompts trip people up in Mind the gaps.
